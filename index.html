Biography

A scientist shaped by curiosity and persistence

Andrzej Lech Ruprecht was a Polish zoologist, osteologist, and long-serving researcher at the Mammal Research Institute of the Polish Academy of Sciences in Białowieża.

Born in Gdańsk on April 20, 1935, Ruprecht began collecting skeletal specimens while still at primary school. After the Second World War, his family returned to Gdańsk. He initially studied medicine, but serious health problems led him to change course. In 1957 he began biology and earth-science studies at Adam Mickiewicz University in Poznań, later transferring to Nicolaus Copernicus University in Toruń. He completed his master’s degree in 1962 with research on the diet of barn owls from the Aleksandrów Kujawski, Ciechocinek, and Raciążek region.

Ruprecht first arrived in Białowieża for an internship on July 1, 1960. In 1964 he joined the Mammal Research Institute permanently, working first as an assistant and later as a curator. He cared for and expanded one of Poland’s largest osteological collections. His research centered on mammalian morphology, systematics, faunistics, and the diagnostic features used to identify skeletal material.

His meticulous analysis of owl pellets and museum collections contributed to major reference works, including Keys to Vertebrates of Poland: Mammals and the Atlas of Polish Mammals. He prepared accounts for 21 bat species and four rodents, collaborated on a comprehensive study of Polish bats, and helped assemble an atlas of Polish owls. His research also documented the giant noctule, Nyctalus lasiopterus, in Poland.

Ruprecht received his doctorate in biological sciences from Maria Curie-Skłodowska University in 1973 for a dissertation on craniometric variation in the muskrat, Ondatra zibethicus. He later broadened his work to Quaternary mammals, building relationships with regional museums and collectors and documenting fossil finds across the Białystok region.

After moving to Ciechocinek with his wife Joanna in 1990, he continued researching, publishing, mentoring younger scientists, protecting bats, and writing for local readers. He authored or co-authored roughly 200 publications, more than 70 of them directly or indirectly concerned with bats. He died in Bydgoszcz on July 2, 2010, and was buried in Włocławek.
